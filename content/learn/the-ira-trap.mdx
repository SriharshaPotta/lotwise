The wash-sale rule usually postpones a loss. There's one place where it deletes the loss instead: your IRA.

## How the trap springs

Normally a disallowed loss moves into the shares you bought back, as a higher cost basis. You get the deduction later, when you sell those shares.

Inside an IRA, cost basis never matters: nothing in the account is taxed when you sell it. So when the replacement shares land in an IRA, the loss has nowhere to go. The IRS settled this in 2008, in Revenue Ruling 2008-5: the loss is disallowed and isn't added to anything. It's simply gone.

## Watch it happen

In the demo portfolio you sold 100 INTC in Brokerage One on September 28 at $21.20. You'd paid $27.40, so that's a $620 loss. 8 days later, the Roth IRA bought 100 INTC. Move the purchase, and switch which account makes it.

Bought back in Brokerage One, the $620 moves into the new shares, like any wash sale. Bought in the Roth IRA, it drops into the pit. Outside the window, in either account, it's deductible this year.

## Why it's easy to fall into

Nobody does this on purpose. It happens because the accounts run separately:

- A retirement plan buys the same fund every month.
- Dividends inside the IRA reinvest automatically.
- A robo-advisor or target-date fund rebalances on its own schedule.
- You harvest a loss in your brokerage account and, the same week, buy the dip in your IRA.

Your broker won't flag it. Even when the IRA is at the same firm, the 1099-B for the taxable account only reports wash sales inside that account.

## How to stay out of it

- Before harvesting a loss, check recent and scheduled IRA purchases of the same security, including automatic ones.
- Pause automatic investments in that security until the window has passed.
- Inside the IRA, buy something similar but not substantially identical while the window is open.
