Most options are taxed like stocks: hold one for a year or less and the gain is short-term. A few kinds, called Section 1256 contracts, play by a different rule. Every gain or loss on them counts as 60% long-term and 40% short-term, however long you held the position. Even a trade that lasted ten minutes.

## Which options get it

Broad-based index options are the common case: options on the S&P 500 index (SPX, and its smaller sibling XSP), the Nasdaq-100 (NDX) and the Russell 2000 (RUT). Regulated futures, and options on them, qualify too. Options on ETFs that track the same index, like SPY or QQQ, do not. They're options on a fund's shares, so the ordinary rules apply.

## Same trade, different tax

In the demo portfolio you closed a SPY call trade for a $7,185 gain after a few weeks. All of it is short-term: at 24%, about $1,724. The same gain on XSP, which tracks the same index, is split 60/40 and comes to about $1,336. That's $388 for choosing the other ticker.

## When it works against you

Switch on "held longer than a year". Now the SPY gain is all long-term at 15%, and XSP still pays its blended 18.6%. Section 1256 helps short-term traders and costs long-term holders.

## Other differences

- Section 1256 positions are marked to market: anything still open on December 31 is taxed as if you'd closed it that day.
- Index options settle in cash, so there are no shares to be assigned.
- A net Section 1256 loss can be carried back up to three years against Section 1256 gains, which ordinary capital losses can't.

## Choosing deliberately

If you trade options on the broad market and close them within a year, the index version of the trade can be cheaper after tax for the same exposure. Contract sizes, liquidity and spreads differ, so it isn't automatic. Lotwise shows both numbers side by side before you trade.
